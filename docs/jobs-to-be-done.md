# Jobs to be done

> Status: approved by Benji Greig, 28 October 2026. Plane: CLR-2. Checked against published design team accounts the same day (see [Sources](#sources)). User stories build on these.

## The main job

**When** I have a brand's colours and need them to work in a product, **I want to** turn them into a complete, accessible colour system I understand, **so I can** design and build with confidence, without working it all out from scratch.

## Supporting jobs

### Getting the system

1. **When** I'm handed a brand palette, **I want to** see how each colour behaves as lighter and darker shades, **so I can** tell early which colours will hold up in an interface and which will struggle.
2. **When** I'm building out shades, **I want** every colour to follow the same lightness steps, **so I can** swap one colour for another and get the same visual weight and contrast.
3. **When** a brand colour is soft or pastel, **I want** its darker shades to keep the brand's character instead of turning muddy, **so I can** use the whole brand, not just its strongest colour.
4. **When** I need colours for errors, warnings and success, **I want** them chosen to stay clearly apart from the brand, **so I can** be sure people never mistake a warning for branding.
5. **When** the product needs light and dark mode, **I want** one set of tokens that works in both, **so I can** support both without designing everything twice.
6. **When** I build a button or any interactive element, **I want** every state it needs (default, hover, pressed, focus, disabled), **so I can** avoid inventing them screen by screen.

### Getting accessibility right

7. **When** I pair two colours, **I want to** know whether they meet contrast requirements before I use them, **so I can** build accessibility in rather than fix it later.
8. **When** I'm short on time, **I want** accessibility to come with the system rather than as an extra step, **so I can** get all of it right, not just the few colours I had time to check.
9. **When** I choose feedback colours, **I want to** know they stay distinguishable for people with colour vision deficiency, **so I can** avoid relying on red against green alone.

### Understanding it

10. **When** the tool makes a choice for me, **I want to** understand why, **so I can** judge it, adjust it, and explain it to my team or client.
11. **When** someone questions a colour decision, **I want** clear reasoning I can point to, **so I can** defend the system with evidence rather than taste.
12. **When** the product palette differs from the brand guidelines, **I want to** show exactly how close it stays to the original, **so I can** bring the brand team with me rather than fight them.

### Using it

13. **When** I've got a system I'm happy with, **I want to** take it into Figma and into code without retyping values, **so I can** keep design and code in step.
14. **When** I or an AI tool am building screens, **I want** tokens with clear names and rules, **so I can** apply colour consistently and get the same result every time.
15. **When** the brand changes or a new colour is added, **I want to** regenerate the system using the same method, **so I can** extend it without starting again.
16. **When** I need to share the colours with people who don't use the tools, **I want** a clear reference of every colour, name and value, **so I can** hand it over as a single document.

## How people want to feel

- **Confident** that the colours will hold up across the whole product, not just the screens they've designed.
- **Capable**, because they understand colour well enough to make good calls themselves.
- **Credible** in front of a client, team or stakeholder, with reasoning to back their choices.

## Who has these jobs

| Who | Main difference |
|---|---|
| **Individual designer** (primary) | Owns the whole job alone, often with no one to check their work |
| **Designer in a small team** | Needs the reasoning to share and agree on with others, including a brand team |
| **Developer, founder or non-designer** | Has the same job with even less colour knowledge to draw on (explainers for them: CLR-65) |
| **Someone working with AI tools** | Needs a method the AI can apply the same way every time |

## Known jobs we're not doing yet

These are real jobs the research confirmed, deliberately left until after the beta (D-023).

- **Data visualisation colours.** **When** I need colours for charts, **I want** a set that's distinct, accessible and doesn't clash with status colours, **so I can** avoid mixing brand meaning with data. (CLR-55)
- **Several brands or themes.** **When** I look after more than one brand or sub-brand, **I want** each palette generated by the same method with the same structure, **so I can** manage them as one system. (CLR-69)

## What the research confirmed

- **Brand palettes are too small for products, and their variants don't form a coherent ramp.** UX Collective, writing for brand designers, says guidelines typically include only a few primary and secondary colours, calls accessibility "the number one reason for color adjustments", and notes that buttons need at least five states the brand palette can't supply. (Jobs 1, 2, 6)
- **Brand colours often fail contrast.** When idealo built dark mode, only three of their five main brand colours passed for text. (Jobs 7, 8)
- **Dark mode is its own problem.** idealo found colours looked muddy on dark surfaces, meeting contrast made blue action text very bright, and icons needed their own colours. (Job 5)
- **Naming by meaning matters.** idealo moved from "red" and "green" to "negative" and "positive", because red and green don't always mean error and success. (Job 14)
- **Late fixes are costly.** Adjustments often surface after hand-off, sometimes after launch, and "impact multiple teams". (Jobs 10, 11, 12)
- **Colour vision deficiency is common.** It affects about 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women, around 300 million people worldwide. (Job 9)
- **Brand colours rarely work for charts.** GoCardless found their brand colours "would lack distinction" in data visualisation, and only a few combinations passed both colour-blindness and contrast checks. (Known job: data visualisation)
